A0.3: An association of co-owners of a residential building installed a video surveillance system (three cameras) in the common parts of the building to protect against vandalism, burglaries, and thefts. A co-owner (TK) objected to the system, claiming it infringed his right to respect for private life, and sought an order requiring the cameras to be removed.

A1.4: The interests must be present and effective as at the date of the data processing and must not be hypothetical at that date, but it cannot be necessarily required that the safety of property and individuals was previously compromised | In this case the condition relating to the existence of a present and effective interest seemed fulfilled since thefts, burglaries and acts of vandalism had occurred before the video surveillance system was installed despite the previous installation of a security system
A1.5: C-212/13 Rynes > "The objective which the controller essentially seeks to achieve when he or she installs a video surveillance system such as that at issue in the main proceedings, namely protecting the property, health and life of the co-owners of a building, is likely to be characterised as a 'legitimate interest', within the meaning of Article 7 of Directive 95/46. A5.2: The Court held that EU law does not preclude national provisions authorising the installation of a video surveillance system in common parts of a residential building for purposes of ensuring safety and protection of individuals and property, without the data subject's consent, provided the three conditions of Article 7(f) of Directive 95/46 are met. The Court outlined the framework for assessing those conditions but left the final determination to the referring court, finding that the interest appeared legitimate in principle, that necessity must be assessed under a strict necessity standard alongside data minimisation, and that balancing must account for the seriousness of the infringement of Charter rights.
